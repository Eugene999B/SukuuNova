# Number Bloom — Game Bible

Status: **Pre-production / vertical-slice candidate**  
Primary audience: **Creche & KG, launch focus age 4–5**  
Secondary expansion: **Lower Primary only after the early-years game proves itself**

---

# 1. Identity

**Title:** Number Bloom  
**Working codename:** Garden Hands  
**Genre:** Touch-first early numeracy toy + guided garden adventure  
**Primary platform/input:** Tablet/phone touch first; mouse fallback; keyboard not required  
**Player fantasy:** I am a little garden keeper whose counting, grouping and pattern-making literally make a magical garden grow.  
**Learning promise:** Children build quantity sense by touching and changing visible groups, not by guessing from text choices.  
**Differentiation:** Number Bloom is a calm direct-manipulation garden. Its main verb is physically arranging quantities, not selecting answers.

## Shipping sentence

> In Number Bloom, the learner is a young garden keeper who repeatedly plants, gathers, splits, matches and arranges visible objects in order to help living garden patches grow. The world responds immediately with growth, movement, character reactions and sound. Learning quantity, number relationships and patterns makes the child better at caring for the garden. They return because the garden remembers their discoveries, new plants and friendly creatures appear, and Free Grow remains a toy they can play with without being graded.

---

# 2. Design pillars

- **Hands before symbols** — children manipulate objects before being expected to reason from numerals alone.
- **The garden is the feedback** — correct mathematical relationships visibly change the world; feedback should not depend on a red/green answer banner.
- **No fear of trying** — there is no countdown, life loss or humiliating wrong-answer state.
- **Voice and pictures first** — a child who cannot yet read should understand the main task.
- **A real toy lives underneath the lessons** — Free Grow and manipulatives must remain enjoyable without a mission prompt.
- **One idea at a time** — the screen never becomes an adult dashboard shrunk for children.

## Anti-pillars

Number Bloom must never become:

- four answer cards with flowers drawn inside them;
- a worksheet with animation;
- a speed-counting test;
- a text-heavy storybook with occasional questions;
- an XP economy that matters more than the garden;
- an interface that requires reading menu labels to progress;
- “harder” only because numbers become larger.

---

# 3. Audience and age-band design

## Launch band — Creche/KG, age 4–5

**Learner profile:** emerging number sense, developing fine motor control, limited reading, strong response to repetition and cause/effect.

**Reading expectation:** none required for core play. On-screen words support adults and emerging readers but narration/icons carry instructions.

**Session length:** 3–8 minutes for guided play; Free Grow can continue longer.

**Input complexity:** tap, drag, drop, trace, slide and simple two-step manipulation.

**Abstraction level:** concrete quantities and spatial relationships first; numerals introduced as labels for groups.

**Scaffolding:** large targets, optional count-along voice, animated pointing/glow, ghost placement only after hesitation or explicit help request.

**Tone:** warm, curious, playful and never baby-talk.

**Failure/recovery:** objects gently return or the garden character reacts with a curiosity cue such as “Let’s count this bed together.” The child can immediately adjust.

**Assessment:** structured manipulation evidence: what was placed, grouped, split, matched or traced; number of self-corrections; whether support was requested.

**Progression:** new garden spaces, plants, insects, weather ambience, manipulatives and playful interactions—not escalating UI complexity.

## Future Lower Primary expansion

Only after the launch slice works with actual children. It may introduce:

- numbers beyond 10/20;
- number bonds;
- repeated groups;
- early addition/subtraction through visible transformations;
- measurement using garden objects;
- simple arrays and shapes.

The interface must mature rather than merely increasing target numbers.

# 4. World and story

## Premise

The Number Bloom Garden is waking after a long dry sleep. Its plants do not need a magical quiz key; they respond to balance, quantity, pattern and care. A small garden guide invites the learner to restore patches one discovery at a time.

## Player role

The player is the **Garden Keeper**. They are trusted because the garden responds to their hands. Adults may call the child by name in narration where privacy/settings permit, but the child is never treated as an employee completing assignments.

## Long-term goal

Restore a ring of small garden zones around the central Bloom Tree. Each zone represents a family of numerical ideas and adds visible life to the home garden.

## Story structure

### Zone 1 — Seedling Patch

Counting, one-to-one correspondence and numeral matching.

### Zone 2 — Twin Beds

More/less/same and visual comparison.

### Zone 3 — Mix & Make Meadow

Compose/decompose quantities: make 5, split 6, combine groups.

### Zone 4 — Pattern Path

Repeating visual patterns and sequence continuation through placement.

### Zone 5 — Harvest Corner

Grouping, sharing and early operation stories using baskets/produce.

Future zones can extend concepts without replacing direct manipulation.

## Characters

### Nia the Sunbird

**Role:** friendly guide and narrator.  
**Personality:** observant, excited by what the child notices, never over-talkative.  
**Gameplay function:** points to task area, counts aloud on request, celebrates strategies.  
**Relationship:** builds a nest near the garden as zones recover.  
**Teaching method:** asks perceptual questions through voice: “Can you touch each seed while we count?” rather than lecturing.

### Kofi the Snail

**Role:** calm retry companion.  
**Gameplay function:** appears when a learner repeatedly mis-groups or rushes, offering a slow count-along.  
**Design reason:** normalizes going slowly without implying failure.

### Momo the Ladybird

**Role:** Free Grow toy companion.  
**Gameplay function:** follows taps, lands on flowers and makes counting play feel alive.

Characters do not become talking answer machines.

# 5. Core gameplay

## Primary verbs

- tap;
- drag;
- place;
- gather;
- split;
- match;
- trace;
- line up;
- pour;
- count/listen.

## 10–60 second loop

1. The child sees one clear garden situation.
2. Nia gives a short spoken goal and the target area pulses once.
3. The child touches or drags real objects in the scene.
4. Objects snap gently into meaningful positions while the game tracks quantity/relationship.
5. The garden responds: seeds sprout, flowers open, baskets fill, creatures move.
6. If the relationship is not yet complete, the child keeps changing the same scene rather than being thrown to a “wrong answer” page.
7. When the relationship is demonstrated, the garden celebrates briefly and opens the next micro-challenge.

## Session loop

A normal guided session contains 3–5 micro-challenges across at most two mechanic families:

- welcome to the current patch;
- play a short manipulation;
- one variation/transformation;
- a transfer task with a changed visual arrangement;
- reward: the patch gains a permanent visual detail;
- child may continue, visit Free Grow or leave.

No default five-question “round.” Session length should reflect attention and mastery, not a universal question count.

## Meta loop

Across days/weeks:

- restore garden zones;
- discover new plant and creature varieties;
- fill a visual Seed Book with things the child has grown;
- revisit old patches with new arrangements;
- unlock playful garden interactions;
- show adults skill evidence quietly outside the child-facing garden.

## Toy test

In **Free Grow**, the child can:

- drag seeds into beds;
- water them;
- tap flowers open/closed;
- gather items into baskets;
- move ladybirds between leaves;
- make groups and hear counts;
- clear/rearrange the plot;
- create simple visual patterns.

No grades, no “correct” configuration, no timer.

If children do not voluntarily play in Free Grow, the core interaction needs improvement before more curriculum is added.

# 6. Launch mechanic families

## 6.1 Plant Count

**Learning target:** one-to-one correspondence and cardinality.

**Setup:** a bed has visible planting holes and a spoken target, for example “Plant five seeds.”

**Action:** drag individual seeds from a seed bowl into holes.

**Feedback:** each accepted seed makes a soft placement sound and can optionally be counted aloud. The total is represented visually, not as an always-present adult counter.

**Completion:** exactly the target quantity is planted and the child indicates done through a large water-can action or the game recognizes completion after a short neutral pause.

**Overfill:** an extra seed remains movable; Nia may say “This bed needs five. Let’s touch the seeds and check.” Nothing explodes and no score is lost.

**Underfill:** the bed simply does not fully bloom yet.

## 6.2 Make the Bed

**Learning target:** composing/decomposing a number.

**Setup:** two small garden beds must together make a target, for example 7.

**Action:** drag flowers/seeds between the two beds, or pour a small group from one container to another.

**Examples:**

- start 5 + 0, make 7 using loose seeds;
- split 6 any way and observe both groups still total 6;
- “Can we make 5 with some yellow and some purple flowers?” multiple solutions accepted.

**Assessment:** recognizes valid mathematical states, including multiple solutions. Do not force one authored combination when several are correct.

## 6.3 Compare Patches

**Learning target:** more, less, same; later numerical comparison symbols only after concrete understanding.

**Setup:** two patches visibly contain groups.

**Action:** the child may line up objects, drag them into paired rows or tap a “pair them” helper. Then they place Nia’s perch beside the group that has more, or place a balance leaf between equal groups.

**Design requirement:** comparison is demonstrated spatially. Do not display “left/right/same” as three textual answer cards.

## 6.4 Match Number Stones

**Learning target:** numeral–quantity association.

**Action:** drag a numeral stone to a garden containing that many objects. Multiple patches may be visible but must remain large and uncluttered.

**Difficulty growth:** change arrangement/subitizing pattern, not only number magnitude.

## 6.5 Pattern Path

**Learning target:** notice and extend repeating patterns.

**Action:** drag flowers/leaves/bugs into empty stepping stones of a garden path.

**Variants:** AB, AAB, ABB, ABC at age-appropriate levels.

**Feedback:** after a valid extension, the path animates as a character walks across the repeated sequence.

## 6.6 Numeral Trace — optional, not core proof

**Learning target:** numeral formation where curriculum/team validates it.

**Action:** trace a broad garden-water path. The goal is motor/number-symbol practice, not a tiny handwriting evaluator.

**Accessibility:** provide non-tracing equivalent if motor needs make tracing unsuitable.

# 7. Rules and state model

## Child-visible state

- current garden zone;
- which patches have bloomed;
- collected plant/creature discoveries;
- current manipulatives;
- optional helper availability.

## Hidden/assessment state

- structured object placements;
- quantity states reached;
- self-correction count;
- hint/support use;
- repeated misconception patterns;
- skill evidence confidence.

## No health bar

There is no garden health meter tied to answer speed. Weather is ambience and story dressing unless a future mechanic explicitly teaches weather/measurement in a non-punitive way.

## Randomness

Randomize visual arrangements and object identities within validated constraints. Do not randomize in ways that alter the mathematical truth or produce perceptually impossible scenes.

---

# 8. Success, failure and recovery

## Local success

The child creates the intended mathematical relationship in the world.

## Mission success

A patch demonstrates several related ideas, including at least one transfer arrangement.

## Long-term success

The garden becomes richer and the learner demonstrates increasingly flexible number relationships.

## Recoverable misconception

The world stays editable. Examples:

- too many seeds: count and remove/move;
- groups not equal: pair them visually;
- pattern mismatch: path pauses at the mismatch and highlights the local relationship, not the “wrong child.”

## Hard failure

None in launch scope.

## Retry cost

Zero.

## Hints

Progressive, strategy-focused:

1. replay spoken goal;
2. pulse manipulable objects/target area;
3. demonstrate a strategy such as touching objects one by one;
4. partial scaffold, never reveal the entire assessed construction unless the game marks the attempt as guided practice rather than mastery evidence.

# 9. Learning architecture

| Concept | In-game action | Evidence of understanding | Common misconception | Feedback/recovery |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One-to-one counting | Place/touch each object once | Target group built without double-counting | Skips or counts an object twice | Optional touch markers/count-along |
| Cardinality | Build a requested group | Stops at the requested amount | Keeps counting without relating last number to total | Garden blooms only when state matches; replay count |
| Numeral matching | Drag numeral stone to quantity | Correct numeral linked across varied arrangements | Chooses by visual size/shape | Re-count objects; compare a known group |
| More/less/same | Pair or align groups, place relationship marker | Correct relationship after direct comparison | Longer-spaced row assumed “more” | Allow objects to be lined up one-to-one |
| Composition | Build target from two groups | Multiple valid decompositions | Believes target has one fixed split | Celebrate several valid ways |
| Pattern | Extend spatial pattern | Correct repeated unit continued | Copies nearest object only | Animate repeated unit / pulse grouping |

## Learning-action test

After learning the concept, the child must be able to **change the garden more intentionally**. “Can select the right option” is not accepted as evidence.

# 10. Mission design grammar

## Mission family A — Build a quantity

Variables: target, object type, spatial arrangement, starting count, number of available objects.

## Mission family B — Transform a quantity

Variables: start state, target state, permitted actions, one or two containers/beds.

## Mission family C — Compare relationships

Variables: group arrangements, visual spacing, equal/unequal states, pairing scaffold availability.

## Mission family D — Continue structure

Variables: pattern unit, object family, empty positions, transformation after success.

### Repetition rules

- same target + same object layout cannot repeat within a short session;
- do not present more than two visually near-identical tasks in succession;
- every assessed sequence should include at least one changed arrangement so rote screen-position memory is insufficient;
- authored narration must remain short and grammatical.

---

# 11. Progression and rewards

Progression is represented in the garden itself.

### Knowledge/mastery

Garden patches develop from seedling → leafy → blooming → diverse ecosystem as the learner demonstrates flexible understanding.

### World

Restore zones around the Bloom Tree.

### Tools

Unlock playful but non-power-creeping tools: watering can skins, basket shapes, new seed trays, pattern ribbons.

### Collection

Seed Book records plants/bugs discovered, each with a simple animated page.

### Customization

Choose flowers, garden signs and path decorations earned through play. Avoid loot-box/random monetization patterns.

### Reward rule

Do not reward speed. Do not reduce rewards because the child revised an arrangement.

# 12. UI/UX Bible

## Presentation metaphor

A garden scene, not a dashboard.

## Main screens

1. Home Garden — visual zone selection.
2. Guided Patch — one focused manipulation.
3. Free Grow — open-ended toy.
4. Seed Book — visual collection.
5. Comfort Settings — adult/assisted access, with child-safe quick audio/replay controls.

## HUD

During active play, only show:

- home/back affordance;
- replay voice button;
- help button where needed;
- current target represented visually/spoken;
- no score wall, timer, XP bar or four stat cards.

## Interaction grammar

- touch object to hear/inspect;
- drag object to meaningful target;
- drop with forgiving snap radius;
- tap group to count aloud when enabled;
- drag between containers to regroup;
- trace broad paths in optional numeral mode.

## First five minutes

1. Nia lands and says a short greeting.
2. A single seed wiggles; child taps it and hears a sound.
3. A bed pulses; child drags one seed into it and sees a sprout.
4. Nia invites “Can we plant three?” Child repeats the action with no menu tutorial.
5. Three planted seeds bloom; Free Grow becomes visible as a picture button.

No modal explaining ten controls.

## Error states

Invalid drops bounce gently back with a neutral sound. The correct destination may pulse only after repeated attempts or help request.

## Responsive behavior

### Tablet

Primary design target. Full garden stage with large draggable objects.

### Phone

One focused bed at a time; manipulatives tray may slide from bottom; never shrink targets below accessibility minimums.

### Desktop

Mouse drag/click supported; no assumption that desktop means more text.

# 13. Art Bible

**Mood words:** warm, tactile, Ghana-friendly tropical garden without reducing the world to national stereotypes, storybook, soft depth, alive.

**Rendering:** 2D illustrated/SVG/DOM sprites for prototype; subtle layered depth.

**Camera:** mostly fixed, close garden diorama; occasional gentle pan between patches.

**Scale:** manipulatives intentionally oversized and readable.

**Silhouettes:** seeds, flowers, leaves, insects and number stones distinguishable by shape as well as colour.

**Animation:** squash/stretch, sprout growth, gentle anticipation; avoid over-stimulation.

**Colour:** cheerful but not neon overload. Mathematical state cannot depend on colour alone.

**Typography:** large rounded readable type, but words are secondary to voice/pictures.

**Do not resemble:** a school quiz portal, spreadsheet, casino reward screen or cyber HUD.

---

# 14. Audio Bible

## Music identity

Light acoustic/percussive garden music with space for narration. Music should feel playful and calm rather than “educational stock music.”

## World sounds

- seed drop;
- soil pat;
- water pour;
- sprout pop;
- flower bloom;
- insect wing/flutter;
- soft basket movement.

## Voice

Narration is important at launch. Requirements:

- short clips;
- replayable;
- natural Ghanaian/region-appropriate voice direction where available;
- no patronizing baby voice;
- subtitles/text equivalent for adults/accessibility.

## Success language

Prefer world reaction first. A bloom/chirp is more important than a voice shouting “CORRECT!”

## Failure language

No buzzer. Use a soft neutral cue for invalid placement.

## Silence strategy

The child may mute music while retaining narration/SFX. The garden remains understandable with audio off through animation/icons.

# 15. Technology plan

## Recommended vertical-slice renderer

Start with **React + SVG/DOM + pointer events**, not Phaser.

Reasons:

- launch mechanics are small numbers of manipulable objects;
- semantic accessibility can be retained more easily;
- direct drag/drop can be implemented with pointer capture and forgiving hit regions;
- bundle weight remains low for early-years mobile devices;
- current product already runs React/Next.

A Canvas/Phaser spike is justified only if:

- richer Free Grow scenes exceed DOM/SVG performance targets;
- many animated entities become core gameplay;
- camera/physics needs become real rather than decorative.

## Input system

Implement game-owned pointer interaction, not shared answer buttons. Support:

- touch;
- stylus where browser exposes it;
- mouse;
- keyboard alternative for accessibility where practical.

## State representation

Structured world state, for example:

```ts
type BloomBedState = {
  id: string;
  slots: Array<{ id: string; occupantId: string | null }>;
};

type BloomAction =
  | { type: "place"; objectId: string; bedId: string; slotId: string }
  | { type: "move"; objectId: string; from: string; to: string }
  | { type: "remove"; objectId: string; from: string }
  | { type: "compare_marker"; relation: "more" | "less" | "same"; targetId: string };
```

Server grader reconstructs relevant mathematical state. No `answer: "7"` shortcut is required for core tasks.

# 16. Accessibility

- 48px+ effective touch targets, larger where possible;
- generous drag hit boxes and snap zones;
- tap-to-select then tap-to-place alternative for children who struggle with drag;
- narration replay at any time;
- colour-independent shapes/status;
- reduced-motion mode replaces bloom animation with simple state change;
- no flashing/high-frequency effects;
- motor alternative to tracing tasks;
- no task depends on hearing alone;
- adult-readable text labels remain available without cluttering child view;
- avoid requiring precision gestures such as pinch unless optional.

---

# 17. Analytics and learning telemetry

Collect only what is useful and privacy-appropriate.

Useful events:

- mechanic entered/completed;
- placement/move/remove action types;
- target state reached;
- help requested;
- count-along used;
- self-correction path;
- invalid-drop rate;
- session exit point;
- Free Grow voluntary engagement;
- device/input mode;
- performance/frame/input latency diagnostics without personally sensitive content.

Key product metrics:

- child completes first task without reading instruction;
- median mis-taps/invalid drops;
- percentage who discover correction without adult intervention;
- percentage who voluntarily enter Free Grow;
- return rate to previously unlocked toy/zone;
- evidence of transfer when layout changes.

Do not optimize for fastest completion.

# 18. Vertical slice specification

The first playable proof should be deliberately small and unusually polished.

## Included

### Mechanic 1 — Plant Count

Targets 1–5 initially, varied spatial layouts, spoken target, direct seed placement.

### Mechanic 2 — Make the Bed

Targets 3–7, two beds, multiple valid compositions.

### Mechanic 3 — Compare Patches

Two groups, pair/line-up strategy, more/less/same through direct manipulation.

### Toy — Free Grow

One open plot with seeds, watering, flowers and baskets. No grade.

## Content volume

Enough authored/generated states to prevent obvious repetition in a 10-minute playtest, not hundreds of shallow tasks.

## Not included yet

- full campaign;
- huge collection system;
- numeral tracing unless basic three-mechanic toy already works;
- lower-Primary arithmetic;
- complex weather systems;
- social/leaderboard features.

---

# 19. Child playtest plan

## Round 1 — interaction comprehension

5–8 representative children if feasible.

Observe silently before helping:

- Can they drag a seed into a bed?
- Do they understand the spoken target?
- Do they count objects one-to-one?
- Do they know how to correct an overfilled bed?
- Are hit targets forgiving enough?
- Do they try Free Grow voluntarily?

## Round 2 — learning transfer

Change visual arrangement without changing concept.

Look for whether children transfer quantity understanding rather than memorize positions.

## Round 3 — delight/replay

After the guided task, ask what they want to do next and watch behavior. Do not ask leading questions such as “Did you like the game?” only.

### Red flags that block promotion

- adult must read every instruction;
- children hunt for an answer button instead of manipulating the scene;
- repeated accidental drops cause frustration;
- child cannot explain/gesture what changed after success;
- Free Grow is ignored because it is less interesting than rewards;
- children rush because interface implies a hidden timer;
- decorative animation hides the mathematical relationship.

---

# 20. Production gate

Number Bloom does not enter full production until the vertical slice demonstrates:

- direct manipulation is more central than text or choices;
- at least three mechanic families feel coherent but distinct;
- a non-reading child can understand the first task through play/voice;
- mistakes are recoverable in place;
- structured actions can be graded server-side without exposing solutions;
- no negative consequence is tied to thinking time;
- touch performance is stable on a realistic lower-power device tier;
- actual children show both comprehension and voluntary replay behavior.

Until then, Number Bloom remains a **prototype candidate**, not a finished flagship.
